Learning to Fly · research notes

The research behind Learning to Fly

Learning to Fly is a free reading game for autistic children. This page sets out what the studies say about teaching reading comprehension to autistic learners, how each finding shapes the app, and where the evidence runs out.

Reviewed 12 October 202630 findings65 sourcesChecked weekly for new studies

The short version

Comprehension can be taught. Software alone rarely teaches it.

Autistic children can learn to understand what they read when teaching is explicit, visual and well prompted. The studies that show this are mostly small: many follow fewer than ten children, and very few meet the What Works Clearinghouse standards for strong evidence. No study has tested a program like this one as a whole.

So the app does three things. It builds each part on a technique that has been tested. It keeps a grown-up in the loop, because the software trials that worked were the ones with an adult beside the child. And it measures its own effect for each learner before anyone claims it works.

Teach it out loud, then fade the help

Every skill is shown, practised with help, then done alone. Hints wait, then step from a light clue to the full answer. R3, R6

Place by profile, not by age

Autistic readers vary from floor to ceiling, so the app checks word reading, reading and listening separately before it starts. R1

Reward effort, never scores

The journey moves with finished sessions, and points come from every answer, right or not. There are no leaderboards, timers or lives. R9

Six numbers

The figures that shaped the design

0.7 SD

How far autistic readers averaged below their peers in comprehension across 36 studies. Individual scores ran from the floor to the ceiling.

So the app places each reader by profile. R1

26 sessions

The one-to-one dose at which a web program raised comprehension. A 16-hour online version of the same program found no gain.

So it is judged after 25 sessions, with an adult involved. R7

.95 vs .80

Effect sizes (Tau-U) in technology-aided reading studies with a wait before the prompt, and without one. The wait was the only feature that mattered.

So the hint wait becomes a fixed, logged rule. R17

1 in 2

Roughly how many autistic participants had marked difficulty naming their own feelings, against about 1 in 20 others.

So understanding a feeling never waits on putting it into words. R15

86%

The share of feelings that autistic teenagers recounting their day mentioned only when asked directly.

So the daily recount always asks how it felt. R20

5%

The share of AI-drafted stories that matched human writers on sentence-to-sentence coherence, though most passed readability checks.

So every drafted text is checked and reviewed. R19

How a session runs

Seven steps, the same every time

A session takes about ten minutes and can be stopped at any point without losing anything. A wrong answer never ends an item or shows a red cross: it opens the next hint.

  1. Plan

    A picture schedule shows today's steps and how many are left.

    R4 · R13

  2. Choose

    The learner picks where to go from two or three places.

    R8

  3. Warm up

    A few quick questions on earlier skills, so they keep coming back.

    R15 · our choice

  4. Watch

    The guide works one example while thinking aloud. It drops out once the skill is shown.

    R3 · R24

  5. Together

    Two or three items where hints wait, then step up.

    R6 · R17

  6. Your turn

    New items done alone. Only these count toward mastery.

    R3 · R12

  7. Finish

    Praise that names the skill, something new for the island, and a talk prompt for the grown-up.

    R9 · R7

Three kinds of reading

Some strands stand on firmer ground than others

The app teaches three kinds of reading, each a region of the learner's island. The evidence behind them is uneven, and the design says so.

Harbor Town

Reading to do

Signs, steps, timetables, labels, messages and on-screen prompts. Each item ends in an action, like choosing the right door.

Solid for words, thin on carry-over

Teaching sight words and warning labels is well established, but few studies checked whether the skill carried into daily life. So every skill is checked in a new setting. R6, R26

Story Woods

Reading to follow a story

Who, where and when, order, causes, pronouns, feelings and the main idea, plus a daily recount of the learner's own day.

The most autism-specific research

Explicit teaching, story maps and pronoun cueing all have autism studies behind them, though most are small. R3, R4, R5

Lantern Peaks

Reading to think about life

Stories and true accounts about joy, loss, fairness, courage and what matters. It opens behind a gate.

The least proven, so it is an experiment

Texts about inner lives are harder for autistic readers, inference is the weak point at this age, and classroom philosophy trials are mixed. R10, R14, R15

The thirty findings

Each study, and what the app does with it

Each finding sets a design rule, numbered R1 to R30. R1 to R16 came from the first evidence review. R17 to R30 came from the October 2026 research scan and are approved features, some already in the app.

In the app built and running Planned approved in the backlog, with its feature number

Placing each reader

Autistic readers differ more from one another than from anyone else. Where a learner starts depends on what they can already do.

R1In the app

Reading profile, not diagnosis, predicts comprehension

Across 36 studies, autistic readers averaged 0.7 standard deviations below their peers, with word-meaning knowledge and decoding the strongest predictors. Individual scores ran from floor to ceiling.

In Learning to FlyA scouting trip checks word reading, reading comprehension and listening comprehension separately, sets a starting step for each strand, and turns on read-aloud when listening beats reading.

Brown et al., 2013 · Nation et al., 2006

R2In the app

Texts about people's minds are harder

Texts that depend on social knowledge are harder for autistic readers than less social texts.

In Learning to FlyEvery passage is tagged for social load, from none to "depends on reading people", and feelings and intentions are taught on purpose rather than assumed.

Brown et al., 2013

R22In the app · LTF-03, 09, 13

The language underneath comprehension

Grammar, listening comprehension and vocabulary predict autistic children's reading comprehension. Readers with weaker working memory or oral language understood more when pronouns were replaced by repeated names, and digital pronoun cues gave higher accuracy than paper ones. Autistic children found "know" against "think" harder than their peers did.

In Learning to FlyA short "Who did it?" grammar stop in scouting, answered by tapping a picture, which can suggest names in place of "he" and "she"; items that set "knows" against "only thinks"; dotted pronouns in guided practice that show who "she" means when tapped; and a setting that repeats names in place of pronouns in the easiest texts.

O'Leary et al., 2026 · Herrero-Roig et al., 2026 · Kritsotakis & Morfidi, 2025 · Courteau et al., 2026 · Jozwik & O'Quin, 2026 · Eilon & Novogrodsky, 2025

Teaching each skill

The strongest single message across the reviews: show the skill, practise it with help, then let the learner do it alone, with pictures doing much of the work.

R3In the app

Explicit teaching works best

Direct, explicit instruction is the most-used method in the intervention studies and is rated highly effective.

In Learning to FlyEvery skill is shown in Watch, practised with help in Together, then done alone in Your turn. The demo drops out once the learner has shown the skill.

Tárraga-Mínguez et al., 2021 · Finnegan & Mazin, 2016 · Steinbrenner et al., 2020

R4In the app

Visual supports carry the most weight

Graphic organizers and visually cued instruction scored highest in two reviews. Story maps and story-grammar icons improved quiz scores and retells in small studies.

In Learning to FlyAnswers are given through story maps, sequence strips and sorting, as the main way to answer rather than an add-on.

Finnegan & Mazin, 2016 · Singh et al., 2021 · Stringfield et al., 2011 · Spencer & Kirby, 2025

R6In the app

Prompting and waiting are established practice

Prompting, time delay, modelling and reinforcement reliably teach sight words, and taught warning-label words along with their meaning. But word-reading studies rarely measured wider literacy, and transfer to new settings was incomplete.

In Learning to FlyHints wait, then step from a light clue to the full answer. Functional words come with their meaning and use, and every skill is checked in a new format or setting before it counts as mastered.

Steinbrenner et al., 2020 · Browder et al., 2006 · Spector, 2011 · Dogoe et al., 2011

R17In the app · LTF-01

The wait is the active ingredient

In 13 quality-screened single-case studies of technology-aided reading, time delay was the only feature that significantly raised effects (Tau-U .95 with it, .80 without). Device, age and outcome type made no difference.

In Learning to FlyThe first guided question on a brand-new skill shows the clue at once, and after that the clue waits as usual. Every guided answer logs its wait, so the learner's own record shows whether it helps.

Kim et al., 2025

R24In the app · LTF-02

Thinking aloud about why people act

Four autistic 10-to-11-year-olds gained 40 to 56 percentage points on questions about why characters say or do things, when teaching combined think-alouds, error-correction prompts and a graphic organizer.

In Learning to FlyOn new feelings questions, the second hint is a three-box card: the clue from the story, what people generally know, and "so", which the learner works out before the answer is shown. The worked example fills in the same card.

Wang & Poon, 2026

R27In the app · LTF-15

On screen is not the same as on paper

Pictures and story maps raised comprehension across 5 single-case studies (Tau-U 0.85), but on-screen versions gave more variable results.

In Learning to FlyA report shows which question types work for each learner, compared only at the same skill and level, and a printable paper story map is offered after story sessions.

Lee, Quinn & Jiang, 2025

Words, pronouns and figures of speech

Some of the most practical gains come from small targets: who a pronoun means, what a word means, and what one small word like "some" does to a sentence.

R5In the app

Cue the pronoun, ask your own questions

Cueing what a pronoun refers to, and having the child generate questions, both helped. With 20 adolescents, pronoun cueing was the only one of three aids with a significant effect.

In Learning to FlyA whole step practises who "he", "she", "it" and "they" mean, with the pronoun marked in the text, and items ask the learner to work with questions about the text.

O'Connor & Klein, 2004 · Whalon & Hanline, 2008

R23In the app · LTF-04

Vocabulary teaching works on a screen

Across 31 studies with 440 autistic students aged 3 to 14, vocabulary teaching worked whether an adult, a computer or a peer gave it, and comprehension improved when it was targeted.

In Learning to FlyTap an underlined word in a harder text for a picture and a plain meaning, read aloud. A warm-up can open with a word for today, from a text coming up or one the learner tapped before. A first set of about 60 texts has word help, to see whether it helps before more are added.

Intepe et al., 2025

R25In the app · LTF-10, 11

"Some" can be harder than a metaphor

On tablet tasks, autistic children matched their peers on metaphor but did worse when "some" meant "not all". Metaphor teaching carried over to new metaphors when it marked what the two things share; idiom teaching stayed item by item.

In Learning to FlyItems built around "some", "only" and "all" in real signs, labels, forms and steps, and a three-box map for metaphors: what it is like, what they share, so what it means. New metaphors without the map show whether it carries over.

Petit et al., 2026 · Skogli-Christensen et al., 2025 · Wei et al., 2025 · Hu et al., 2025

Feelings and inference

Reading about what people feel and want is the hard part for older autistic readers, and it is the doorway to the thinking strand.

R14In the app

Inference lags behind retelling

In 81 autistic 8-to-16-year-olds, retelling was a relative strength while inference fell far below average, and both predicted later reading comprehension. In another study, autistic readers made emotion inferences while reading but struggled to answer direct questions about them.

In Learning to FlyUnderstanding is checked by choosing and pointing before any explaining. The thinking strand opens only after an inference check on stories the learner has never seen.

McIntyre et al., 2020 · Tirado & Saldaña, 2016

R15In the app

Putting feelings into words is hard for many

About half of autistic participants, against about 5% of others, scored as having marked difficulty identifying and describing their own feelings. Across 22 trials, taught emotion and perspective skills often did not last or carry over, and the evidence was low quality.

In Learning to FlyUnderstanding and expressing are separate tiers. Expressing is supported and tracked but never required to move on, and every mastered skill comes back for review.

Kinnaird et al., 2019 · Fletcher-Watson et al., 2014

Telling about today

Saying what happened at school is a skill of its own. The story map that organizes a story can organize a day, so every narrative step is practised twice: once on a text, once on the learner's own day.

R16In the app

Telling about one's day can be taught

Autistic 8-to-17-year-olds recalled fewer specific personal memories than matched peers and needed more prompts, though memory for recent events was intact. Children who retold model stories and then their own, with visual support faded out, improved their personal narratives, though the gains held unevenly.

In Learning to FlyA daily "Today" recount: the school timetable as cards, specific questions answered with tiles, sentences built from the answers, a prompt card for the grown-up, and supports faded slowly.

Goddard et al., 2014 · Petersen et al., 2014 · Gauvreau, 2015

R20In the app · LTF-05, 06

Ask what happened next, and how it felt

Autistic 11-to-17-year-olds journaling their day gave the setting and the main event unprompted, but about 60% of what happened next and 86% of feelings came only when asked. A four-panel strip helped them remember.

In Learning to FlyToday asks "How did you feel?", and "What happened after that?" after anything hard, and later also after what the learner did. The day then shows as four boxes, before, what happened, after, and how it felt, and tapping a box changes it.

Yang et al., 2026

R28In the app · LTF-07, 08

Count how much is said, and fade the cues

Autistic adolescents' retells looked less structured, but the gap mostly disappeared once length was taken into account. Printed cues helped a child report past events, but they were never removed, so no one knows whether the skill held without them.

In Learning to FlyThe grown-up sees how long each recount is next to which parts of the day were told, the questions skipped most, and how often the learner adds their own words. Later stages put the tiles behind a "Show me ideas" button, count answers given without them, and step back a stage if the ideas are needed every time for two weeks.

Harvey et al., 2025 · McWilliams & Hanson, 2025

R21In the app · LTF-14

A good retell is not good reading

In a randomized trial with 21 autistic students, oral narrative training improved spoken storytelling and perspective-taking, but not reading comprehension.

In Learning to FlyStory skills are practised on written text, and about every ten sessions an optional check-up flight measures reading on six new texts themed to where the learner is on the journey, with no hints and no right or wrong shown. Results are kept apart from retell and from practice, and never change a skill.

Masoumi et al., 2024

Reading to do

Functional reading matters most when it changes what the learner does next.

R26In the app · LTF-12

Understanding a message is not acting on it

In a randomized pilot, young adults with intellectual or developmental disabilities taught to read texts and emails improved in comprehension, but not in replying to them.

In Learning to FlyMessage items end with a second step on the same message: pick the reply that matches the plan, or tap what to do. The report shows understanding and acting as separate lines.

Prahl et al., 2025

Software, motivation and the game

The software trials that helped were the ones that carried good teaching with an adult beside the child. The game around it is there to bring the learner back, not to teach.

R7In the app

Software carries teaching; it does not replace it

One web program raised comprehension over 26 one-to-one sessions, but not in school groups or in a 16-hour online version. Pooled effects of group studies are small. A parent-led shared-reading program improved story comprehension in 31 preschoolers.

In Learning to FlyA grown-up stays in the loop: a talk prompt after every session, a dashboard and a printable report, and at least 25 sessions before anyone judges whether it works.

Bailey et al., 2017 · Arciuli & Bailey, 2019 · Bailey et al., 2022 · Zhang et al., 2023 · Grindle et al., 2020 · Lo & Shum, 2021

R8In the app

Interests: test them, don't assume them

Putting one child's special interest into the text improved that child's answers and retell. A later pair of studies with interest-based text gave mixed results.

In Learning to FlyTexts lean toward the learner's interests, but one pick in five ignores them on purpose, and the grown-ups' area compares the two.

El Zein et al., 2016 · Solis et al., 2022

R9In the app

Reward effort, not performance

Expected tangible rewards for performance reduce later free-choice interest, while positive feedback raises it. Gamification has small positive effects on learning, and a game story helps behaviour.

In Learning to FlyPraise names the skill. The journey moves with finished sessions, flight points come from every answer whether right or not, and there are no leaderboards, timers, lives or breakable streaks.

Deci et al., 1999 · Sailer & Homner, 2020

R30In the app

The game is the wrapper, not the lesson

Across 24 randomized trials, game-based interventions for autistic children improved social skills and thinking skills but not language, and the social gains were not significant for adolescents.

In Learning to FlySea routes to islands themed on the learner's interests stay a wrapper: on the way, practice leans toward the island's topics, and the lessons do the teaching. Reading gains are expected from the lessons, and the game never stands in for them.

Gao et al., 2025

R13In the app

Predictable, adjustable, and designed with children

Design guidance for autistic children's software centres on adapting to the individual, controlling sensory stimulation and keeping the interface predictable. Autistic children can take part in design critique when given the right tools.

In Learning to FlyThe layout never moves. Sound, motion, colour theme, text size and read-aloud speed are adjustable, and everything can be done by tapping alone.

Davis et al., 2010 · Sanmartin et al., 2026 · Frauenberger et al., 2013

R29In the app · LTF-18

Ask the learner, with pictures

A review of 24 studies found autistic children took part in research best through sorting, pictures and making things, and were rarely asked to review changes.

In Learning to FlyAfter the learner first uses something new, a one-time card asks "What do you think?" with picture buttons, then whether there is too much of it, just right or too little. The grown-up sees the answers next to each feature.

Johnson et al., 2026

The thinking strand

Reading about joy, loss and fairness is the stretch goal. It is treated as an experiment and earns its place in each learner's own record.

R10In the app

Philosophy with children has mixed evidence

A 48-school trial estimated about two months' extra progress in reading; a 198-school trial found no effect on attainment. One small Scottish project reported autistic pupils taking full part in structured inquiry.

In Learning to FlyLantern Peaks opens only after the core skills are mastered and have lasted, takes at most one session in four, and scores reasoning on a four-step rubric. Hard themes such as loss stay off until a grown-up turns them on.

EEF, 2015 · EEF, 2021 · Cassidy et al., 2013

AI behind the scenes

A language model helps draft content ahead of time. It never talks to the learner and never decides mastery.

R11In the app

AI drafts; people and rules decide

A large model scored children's short reading answers near expert agreement in one study, but a 2026 meta-analysis found such scoring weak overall, and models asked to simplify text struggled to reach a 4th-grade level.

In Learning to FlyAI-drafted passages pass a reading-level check and a person's review. Mastery comes only from structured items scored by fixed rules.

Henkel et al., 2023 · Hardy, 2026 · Day et al., 2025

R18Planned · LTF-17

Scoring slips on partly right answers

AI scorers agree with people on clearly right or wrong answers but slip on partly right ones and on answers people score differently. A 3-level retell rubric reached 0.78 agreement with large hosted models.

PlannedBefore any typed answer is scored, a test set checks agreement separately for clearly right, partly right and clearly wrong answers, with a pass mark in each.

Henkel et al., 2025 · Gurin Schleifer et al., 2026

R19Planned · LTF-16

Readable is not the same as coherent

Generated text passes surface checks but misses elsewhere: only 5% of AI-drafted stories matched human writers on sentence-to-sentence coherence, and only 42.6% of generated questions were the inference type asked for. Small fine-tuned models reached early-grade readability better than large models given no examples.

PlannedNew content gets coherence and skill-fit checks as well as the reading-level check, and near-duplicate passages are flagged.

Maheshwary et al., 2026 · Ma et al., 2025 · Shen et al., 2026

Measuring itself

Because the evidence is thin, the app has to show its own effect.

R12In the app

The evidence base is thin

Most reading-comprehension studies with autistic learners are small single-case studies, and very few meet What Works Clearinghouse standards.

In Learning to FlyA starting point, a record per skill, reviews at about 3, 7 and 21 days, and reports in the grown-ups' area, before anyone claims the app works.

Singh et al., 2021 · Zhang et al., 2023

Our own choices

Decisions no study made for us

Some parts of the design are judgement calls, not tested rules. They are listed here so they can be checked against each learner's results and changed if they are wrong.

  • Mastery80% right on the learner's own across two sessions on different days, including one item in a new format or setting.
  • ReviewsAt about 3, 7 and 21 days. A missed review sends the skill back to practice, and nothing already earned is taken away.
  • Text levelsFive per skill. Two first-try answers in a row move up a level, two misses move down, and the hardest levels open only by climbing.
  • Fast trackOne flawless session with a new format and a harder text can master a skill. Reviews still check that it lasts.
  • The gate to Lantern PeaksFive narrative steps and three functional steps mastered and lasting, plus 80% on an inference check with unseen stories.
  • Demos that drop outOnce the learner has shown a skill, the worked example is skipped, and every demo has a "Got it, skip" button.
  • Setbacks on the journeyA storm or a broken propeller, each with a calm coping line and a choice of plan, to practise staying steady when plans change.
  • Points and nudgesFlight points for every answer, right or not. After a long pause the guide offers to read aloud or give a hint.

How we will know

One learner first, then a pilot, then a public release

The app is built first with one learner at home. It runs as a single-case study: a starting point first, then skills introduced one at a time, so the record shows which skills moved and when. The app also tracks which questions each learner has already seen, so new features are measured only on questions the learner has not seen before.

  1. 1
    FoundationsEvidence review, family co-design, the skill map and the open content sources.Gate: a specialist signs off the skill map.
  2. 2
    Evals firstGold test items, AI evaluation sets, failure modes and a privacy review.Gate: the AI meets its pass marks and the consent flow is reviewed.
  3. 3
    First sliceFunctional and narrative skills, the lesson loop, the island and a basic dashboard.Gate: children finish sessions and ask to come back.
  4. 4
    Full buildAll 18 skills, the content bank, the adaptive engine and interest topics.Gate: every skill and level has checked content.
  5. 5
    PilotProposed: 8 to 12 children for 8 to 12 weeks, each with a starting point, weekly probes per skill, and a standard comprehension test before and after.Gate: gains on an outside reading measure, then public release.

Content and privacy

Free to read is not the same as free to reuse

Every passage comes from public-domain or openly licensed sources, or is written fresh from facts, and each one carries its own credit line. The content bank is published under CC BY-SA 4.0, so anyone can reuse it, and the code is under the MIT License.

Several popular free resources are free to read but not free to adapt, and some now bar AI processing in their terms, so they are left out. Examples are Khan Academy, CK-12, OpenStax (CC BY-NC-SA since April 2026), ReadWorks and CommonLit.

What the app keeps, and where

Every answer is kept on the learner's device; a signed-in family can also keep a private cloud copy. A child profile is a nickname and a guide, with no real name, photo, location or diagnosis. There are no analytics, ads or third-party scripts, and no AI talks to the child.

This page follows the same rule: it loads no outside scripts, and its fonts are served from this site.
